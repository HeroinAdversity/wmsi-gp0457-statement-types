The Eight Terms

The syllabus names eight terms candidates should understand when analysing sources. Study the definition, signal words, and example for each — then note what it is commonly confused with.

How to use this tab: Read each card carefully. Pay special attention to the Signal Words — these are your shortcuts in the exam. The Don't confuse with note targets the most common student mistake for each type. 仔细阅读每张卡片。特别注意"信号词"——它们是考试中的快捷方式。
FACT 事实

Definition: Something that is known or can be proved to be true.

Signal words: statistics, named sources, dates, measured data, "according to [named source]", "data shows"

"Malaysia's unemployment rate was 3.6% in 2024, according to the Department of Statistics."

⚠ Don't confuse with Claim — a claim ASSERTS something is true but may lack verifiable evidence.

OPINION 意见

Definition: A personal view or judgement not based on fact or knowledge.

Signal words: "I think", "I believe", "I feel", "in my view", "it seems to me", "personally"

"I think schools should spend more time teaching practical life skills."

⚠ Don't confuse with Value Judgement — a value judgement specifically assesses something as good, bad, right, or wrong.

PREDICTION 预测

Definition: What someone thinks may happen in the future.

Signal words: "will", "could", "is likely to", "by 2030", "in the future", "if…then", "may lead to"

"If deforestation continues at this rate, the Amazon rainforest could disappear within 80 years."

⚠ Don't confuse with Claim — a claim is about the present or past, a prediction is about the future.

VALUE JUDGEMENT 价值判断

Definition: An assessment of something as good or bad in terms of someone's standards or priorities.

Signal words: "should", "must", "the best", "the worst", "wrong", "right", "unfair", "essential", superlatives, moral language

"Access to clean water is the most important human right."

⚠ Don't confuse with Opinion — an opinion is a personal preference ("I like…"), while a value judgement makes a moral or evaluative claim about what is good/bad/important.

GENERALISATION 概括

Definition: A statement suggesting something is true all of the time when it is only true some of the time, or applying the traits of a small group to a whole population.

Signal words: "all", "every", "always", "never", "everyone", "no one", "completely", absolute language

"Young people today are all addicted to their phones and never read books."

⚠ Don't confuse with Claim — a claim may be debatable but doesn't necessarily use absolute "all/every/always" language.

CLAIM 主张

Definition: A statement or assertion about an issue that is presented as true. It may or may not be supported by evidence.

Signal words: assertions without "I think", presented as if true, debatable statements, "research shows" (without naming the research)

"Social media causes anxiety in teenagers."

⚠ Don't confuse with Fact — a fact can be verified; a claim is asserted but not necessarily proved.

BIAS 偏见

Definition: Being unfairly prejudiced for or against something. Shown through language, selection of evidence, or one-sided presentation.

Signal words: emotive language, loaded words ("reckless", "flood", "destroy"), exaggeration, one-sided arguments, ignoring counter-evidence

"The government's reckless spending has destroyed the economy, and only a fool would support these policies."

⚠ Don't confuse with Vested Interest — bias is about HOW something is said (the language); vested interest is about WHO is saying it (their personal stake).

VESTED INTEREST 利益相关

Definition: A personal reason for involvement in an issue, especially expecting financial or other gain.

Signal words: look at WHO is speaking — company CEO, industry spokesperson, government official assessing their own policy, someone who benefits financially

"The CEO of a solar panel company argues that every home should be required to install solar panels."

⚠ Don't confuse with Bias — having a vested interest doesn't automatically mean the statement is wrong, but it means the speaker may not be objective.

Exam tip — the 3-question test: When you read a statement in the exam, ask yourself:
1. Can I verify this? → If yes, it's probably a Fact.
2. Is this about the future? → If yes, it's probably a Prediction.
3. Does it use "all/every/always/never"? → If yes, it's probably a Generalisation. 考试技巧：三步测试法 — 能否验证？是否关于未来？是否使用"所有/每个/总是/从不"？

In Malaysia, the manufacturing sector employs 2.7 million workers, many of whom perform routine tasks that could be automated.

Confusable Pairs Clinic

These four pairs cause the most mistakes in exams. Study the key difference, then sort the practice statements.

Pair 1: Fact vs Claim

FACT 事实

Can be verified — checked against a reliable source, measured, or proved. Often includes specific numbers, dates, or named sources.

CLAIM 主张

Asserted as true but is debatable. May sound factual but cannot be simply checked — it requires argument and evidence to support.

The test: Ask — "Could I look this up in a reliable source and get a definite yes/no answer?" If yes → Fact. If it needs debate or argument → Claim.

Sort these statements — is each one a Fact or a Claim?

Pair 2: Opinion vs Value Judgement

OPINION 意见

A personal view — the speaker's own preference, feeling, or belief. Signalled by "I think", "I believe", "I feel".

VALUE JUDGEMENT 价值判断

An evaluative assessment — something is judged as good/bad, right/wrong, important/unimportant. Uses moral or priority language.

The test: Ask — "Is the speaker just sharing what they personally prefer, or are they making a judgement about what is right/wrong/best/worst?" Personal preference → Opinion. Moral or evaluative ranking → Value Judgement.

Pair 3: Generalisation vs Claim

GENERALISATION 概括

Applies something to ALL cases when it's only true for some. Uses absolute language: all, every, always, never, no one.

CLAIM 主张

Asserts something as true but is debatable. Does NOT necessarily use absolute language — it may use hedging words like "some", "often", "tends to".

The test: Look for absolute words (all/every/always/never). If present → probably a Generalisation. If the statement is debatable but doesn't use absolutes → probably a Claim.

Pair 4: Bias vs Vested Interest

BIAS 偏见

Shown through HOW something is said — emotive language, loaded words, one-sided presentation, exaggeration.

VESTED INTEREST 利益相关

About WHO is saying it — the speaker has a personal or financial reason for their position. Look at their role or identity.

The test: Ask two separate questions: (1) "Does the LANGUAGE show unfairness?" → Bias. (2) "Does the SPEAKER benefit from this view?" → Vested Interest. A statement can show BOTH.

For each statement, decide: is the main feature Bias (language) or Vested Interest (who benefits)?

QUESTION 1
Using Source A, identify one fact about automation and employment.
[1 mark]
What to look for: A fact is something that can be verified — look for specific numbers, named organisations, or measurable data. Ask yourself: "Could I check this in a report or database?"

Signal words in Source A: percentages, named organisations (ILO), specific figures (85 million, 2.7 million, 340,000, 42%).

Sentence starter: "One fact from Source A is that…"
MODEL ANSWER

"Automation has already displaced approximately 85 million jobs worldwide since 2020" (ILO report).

✓ This works because it cites a specific figure from a named source (the ILO), making it verifiable. Other acceptable answers: "the manufacturing sector employs 2.7 million workers" or "340,000 workers enrolled in digital skills training programmes last year, a 42% increase from the previous year."

QUESTION 2
Using Source B, identify one opinion expressed by Mei Ling.
[1 mark]
What to look for: An opinion is a personal view — not something that can be proved true or false. Look for phrases where Mei Ling shares what she personally thinks or feels.

Signal words to find: "I think", "I believe", "I feel", "in my view".

Sentence starter: "One opinion expressed by Mei Ling is…"
MODEL ANSWER

Mei Ling says "I think automation is unfair to workers because companies only care about profits."

✓ The phrase "I think" is the signal. This is her personal view, not a verifiable fact. Note: "companies only care about profits" is also a generalisation embedded within the opinion — but the question asks for an opinion, and the "I think" marker makes this the clearest example.

QUESTION 3
Using Source A, identify one generalisation.
[1 mark]
What to look for: A generalisation says something is true for ALL/EVERY/ALWAYS when it's only true for some. Scan Source A for absolute words.

Signal words to scan for: "all", "every", "always", "never", "everyone", "no one", "completely".

Tip: There are several generalisations in Source A. The clearest ones contain the words "all", "every", "always", or "completely".
MODEL ANSWER

"All the new jobs require university degrees, so ordinary workers are being left behind completely."

✓ The words "All" and "completely" are the markers. Other acceptable answers: "Every business that uses our systems has grown its workforce" (the word "every") or "People always resist change, but progress is inevitable and always beneficial" (the word "always" used twice).

QUESTION 4
Explain why your answer to Question 3 is a generalisation.
[2 marks]
What the examiner wants (2 marks):
Mark 1: Show you understand what a generalisation IS (the definition).
Mark 2: Explain specifically HOW your chosen statement fits that definition — point to the exact word(s) that make it a generalisation, and explain why the statement is only true for SOME, not ALL.

Sentence frame:
"This is a generalisation because it suggests that [what the statement says is true for all] when in reality [it is only true for some]. The word '[signal word]' makes it a generalisation because [explain why the absolute language is an over-claim]."

Common mistake: Simply quoting the statement again without explaining WHY it is a generalisation — this earns 0 marks. You must use the word "generalisation" and explain what makes it one. 常见错误：仅引用原文而不解释为什么它是概括——这将得0分。你必须解释是什么使它成为概括。
MODEL ANSWER (using "All the new jobs require university degrees…")

This is a generalisation because it suggests that every single new job created by automation requires a university degree, which applies the experience of some workers to all workers. The word "all" makes it a generalisation because in reality, some new jobs — such as machine maintenance, data entry, or warehouse logistics — may not require a university degree. The word "completely" also generalises by suggesting that every ordinary worker without a degree is being entirely excluded from the job market, when some may have successfully retrained or found alternative employment.

✓ This answer scores 2/2 because it: (1) defines what a generalisation does (applies something to all when true for some), and (2) explains specifically which words ("all", "completely") create the generalisation and gives a concrete counter-example showing it's not universally true.

QUESTION 5
Using both Source A and Source B, identify one person or group who might have a vested interest in the automation debate. Explain what their vested interest is and how this might affect the reliability of their statement.
[3 marks]
What the examiner wants (3 marks):
Mark 1: Identify WHO has a vested interest (name or role).
Mark 2: Explain WHAT their vested interest is — what do they personally gain?
Mark 3: Explain HOW this vested interest might affect the reliability of what they say.

Step-by-step approach:
1. Scan for speakers — who is quoted? What is their job/role?
2. Ask "who benefits?" — does this person gain money, power, or reputation if their view is accepted?
3. Link to reliability — explain why their personal benefit might make them present information in a one-sided way.

Sentence frame:
"[Name/role] has a vested interest because [what they gain]. This means their statement that '[brief quote]' may not be fully reliable because [how the interest could make them one-sided]."

Candidates in the sources with potential vested interests: Mr Tan Wei Jie (CEO of RoboTech Solutions), Minister Saravanan (assessing his own government's programmes), technology companies (mentioned by Mei Ling). 思路：找到发言人→问"谁受益？"→解释利益如何影响可靠性。
MODEL ANSWER

Mr Tan Wei Jie, the CEO of RoboTech Solutions, has a vested interest because his company manufactures and sells automation technology. He benefits financially if more businesses adopt automation, so he has a strong personal reason to promote it.

This means his statement that "our technology creates far more jobs than it replaces" may not be fully reliable, because he is unlikely to acknowledge any negative effects of automation if doing so would discourage potential customers from buying his products. His claim that "every business that uses our systems has grown its workforce" may be selectively presented — he would not publicise cases where businesses reduced their workforce after adopting his technology.

✓ This answer scores 3/3 because it: (1) names the person and their role, (2) clearly explains the financial gain (selling automation products), and (3) explains how this interest could make the statement unreliable (selective presentation, unlikely to acknowledge negatives). Note: Datuk Seri Saravanan also has a vested interest — as a government minister, he has a political interest in presenting his own department's programmes positively.
