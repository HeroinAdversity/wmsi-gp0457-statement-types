# Types of Statements — Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives 0457
## Source brief for Year 10, Term 1, Weeks 2–3

### Why this topic matters

The Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus names **eight terms** that candidates should understand when reading exam sources:

**bias, claim, fact, generalisation, opinion, prediction, value, and vested interest.**

One of these — **generalisation** — is tested directly in the exam. The other seven sharpen a student's reading for Questions 2, 3 and 4.

### THE PRIORITY: Generalisation and Question 1(b)

This is the single most important part of this lesson and should receive the greatest emphasis.

- **Question 1(b)(i) — 1 mark.** The student is given three statements from a source and must identify which one *is* a generalisation. There is one correct answer only.
- **Question 1(b)(ii) — 2 marks.** The student must explain **why** their chosen statement is a generalisation. The explanation must link clearly either to (a) a small sample being applied to everyone, or (b) an "always/never" claim that is not always true.

That is **3 marks available in the first five minutes of the paper.** Students who recognise the pattern bank these marks quickly and move on with confidence.

---

## The eight terms in detail

All examples below are built around a single strand: **youth mental health and social media use.** An important teaching point is that *the wording is what signals the statement type — not the topic.*

---

### 1. GENERALISATION (概括性陈述) — tested directly in Q1(b)

**Definition:** A statement that takes something true of a **small group or a specific case** and presents it as true for **everyone** — or uses an absolute word like *always*, *never*, or *all* when the truth is only *sometimes*.

**✓ This IS a generalisation:**
> "A survey of 200 students in one city found 60% felt anxious after using social media — so teenagers everywhere are being harmed by their phones."

**✗ This is NOT a generalisation:**
> "A 2024 survey of 200 students in one city found that 60% reported feeling anxious after extended social media use."

**Why the difference matters:** The second sentence is just a **fact**. It stays honest about its sample size — 200 students, one city — and doesn't stretch the claim any further. The first sentence takes that same small sample and applies it to *"teenagers everywhere."* **That stretch is the generalisation.**

**Signal words to watch for:** always · never · everyone · all [group] · no one · every time

---

### 2. FACT (事实) — appears in Q2, Q3, Q4 as evidence

**Definition:** A statement that can be checked and shown to be true or false using evidence.

**✓ Fact:**
> "The World Health Organization reported a rise in reported cases of teen anxiety between 2015 and 2023."

**✗ Not a fact — it's an opinion:**
> "Social media is the worst thing that has ever happened to young people."

**Key nuance:** A fact can still be selectively chosen or presented with bias — being factual does not automatically mean a source is balanced.

---

### 3. OPINION (观点) — appears in Q2, Q3 for evaluating reasoning

**Definition:** A personal view or judgement that cannot be proven true or false. It can be reasonable or unreasonable, but it cannot be "checked."

**✓ Opinion:**
> "I think schools should ban phones completely — it's the only way students will focus."

**✗ Not an opinion — it's a value:**
> "Every young person deserves the right to switch off without judgement."

**Key nuance:** Opinions and values overlap. The difference is that a **value** expresses a *principle* about what matters, while an **opinion** is usually a specific judgement or recommendation.

---

### 4. CLAIM (主张) — appears in Q3 for analysing arguments

**Definition:** An assertion put forward as true. It might be backed by evidence, or it might not be. The word "claim" by itself does not tell you whether it is reliable.

**✓ Claim (unsupported):**
> "App developers claim their new 'focus mode' reduces screen time by half."

**A claim WITH evidence becomes stronger:**
> "App developers' internal data, reviewed by an independent auditor, showed a 48% reduction in screen time among test users."

**Key nuance:** The student's job when evaluating a claim is to ask: *what evidence backs this up, and who is making it?*

---

### 5. VALUE (价值观) — appears in Q3, Q4 for perspectives

**Definition:** A statement reflecting what someone believes is important, right, fair, or desirable — underlying principles rather than facts.

**✓ Value:**
> "Protecting children's mental health should always come before a company's profit."

**✗ Not a value — it's a prediction:**
> "If nothing changes, mental health referrals will keep rising over the next decade."

---

### 6. PREDICTION (预测) — appears in Q2, Q4 for future consequences

**Definition:** A statement about what is expected to happen in the future. It cannot yet be checked against evidence.

**✓ Prediction:**
> "Experts warn that without regulation, screen-related anxiety cases will double by 2030."

**✗ Not a prediction — it's a fact (past tense, checkable now):**
> "Screen-related anxiety cases doubled between 2015 and 2023."

---

### 7. BIAS (偏见) — appears in Q2 for evaluating research

**Definition:** A one-sided slant in how information is selected or presented — usually favouring one perspective while downplaying or omitting others.

**✓ Shows bias:**
> A wellness-app company's blog only cites studies where its product improved user mood, and never mentions studies with no effect.

**✗ Balanced — not biased:**
> A university research summary presents both studies that found benefits and studies that found no significant effect.

**Key nuance:** Bias is about **selection** — what's left in, and what's left out — not necessarily about lying.

---

### 8. VESTED INTEREST (既得利益) — appears in Q2, Q3 for source reliability

**Definition:** A personal, financial, or professional stake that gives someone a reason to present information in a way that benefits them.

**✓ Vested interest present:**
> A company that sells "digital detox" retreats publishes an article on the dangers of phone use.

**✗ No obvious vested interest:**
> An independent, government-funded health body publishes findings on phone use and anxiety.

**Key nuance:** A vested interest doesn't automatically make a source wrong — but it is a reason to check the evidence more carefully.

---

## The commonly confused pairs

These are the distinctions students most often get wrong, and are worth dwelling on:

1. **Generalisation vs. Fact** — Does the statement stay honest about its sample, or does it stretch to "everyone"?
2. **Opinion vs. Value** — Is it a specific judgement, or an underlying principle about what matters?
3. **Prediction vs. Fact** — Is it about the future (uncheckable now), or the past (checkable now)?
4. **Bias vs. Vested Interest** — Bias is in the *presentation*; vested interest is in the *stake the person holds*.
5. **Claim vs. Fact** — A claim is asserted; a fact is verifiable. A claim may turn out to be either true or false.

## The exam takeaway

For Question 1(b), the student should run this two-step check on each of the three candidate statements:

**Step 1 — Look for the stretch.** Does the statement take a small group, a single study, or one case and apply it to a much larger group?

**Step 2 — Look for the absolute.** Does it use *always*, *never*, *everyone*, *all*, or *no one* where the reality is only *sometimes*?

If either is true, it is a generalisation. Then in 1(b)(ii), the student must say *which* of those two things it does, and name the specific stretch or the specific absolute word.
